ST-VE-001 · GENERAL NOTES

Where the heat goes

  1. THE BOXAn underground station is a box buried in soil with very little natural ventilation. Almost every watt used inside it ends up as heat in the air, so the station's ventilation is really a machine for carrying that heat back up to the street.
  2. HEAT SOURCESAt peak, the biggest source is the trains: braking and traction losses, and the condensers of their air-conditioning. Passengers, lighting, escalators and equipment rooms add the rest. At night, with no trains, the station still runs lights, fans and pumps.
  3. PISTON EFFECTA train in a tunnel works like a piston. It pushes a slug of air ahead of it and drags air in behind it. That free air movement helps ventilate the tunnels while trains are running.
  4. TRACKWAY EXHAUSTDucts over the track (over-track exhaust, OTE) pull hot air from the brakes and AC condensers before it reaches the platform, and push it up the vent shafts. Platform screen doors keep the tunnel air apart from the platform air.
  5. STATION COOLINGAir-handling units in the plant rooms supply cooled air to the concourse and platform.
  6. TVS PLANTEach station end has a fan room with two reversible tunnel ventilation fans (TVFs). The air path is: tunnel → nozzle damper (NZD) or tunnel damper (SRD) → fans → fan isolation damper (FID) → vent shaft → shaft damper (SD) → street. A draught relief damper (DRD) links the tunnel straight to the shaft and bypasses the fans. In supply, a Saccardo nozzle jets air along the tunnel to raise its velocity. The adjacent station has the same plant. Here it is drawn close and simplified; in reality it is typically around a kilometre away. PRACTICE
  7. NORMAL MODEThe TVFs are off. DRD and SD stay open, so the piston effect breathes tunnel air in and out through the shafts (normal-open). When the outside air is hot, SD can close so the air recirculates instead (normal-closed, not shown). PRACTICE
  8. CONGESTION MODEIf a train stops in the tunnel, the piston effect stops with it, while the train's AC keeps rejecting heat. As the air at the AC condensers nears about 46 °C, the TVS starts push-pull: the station nearer the train supplies and the farther one extracts. PRACTICE
  9. EMERGENCY MODEFor a train fire, the airflow direction is set by where the fire is, so that smoke goes away from the side passengers evacuate to. The airflow must reach at least the critical velocity, which stops smoke creeping back under the crown (backlayering), and full mode must be reached within 180 s (NFPA 130 2017 §7.2.1). Critical velocity depends on the design fire size, which is a project value, so no number is shown. CODE
  10. CODE BASISNFPA 130 (2017) Chapter 7 sets the emergency ventilation requirements: airflow held for at least 1 h, and fans rated for the hot-gas temperature found by analysis, not less than 150 °C. NBC 2016 has no trainway ventilation section, so Indian practice followed NFPA 130 and specified fans at 250 °C for 2 h. NBC 2026 Annex J-6 adds a trainway emergency ventilation section, including a 2 h airflow duration. CODE
  11. CODE & PRACTICE CHECK
  12. THE FLOWSoft puffs of air drift along the air paths at the air speed. Their colour and density show how concentrated the heat is: dense red where the heat is concentrated (brakes, AC condensers, a stalled train), thinning through orange and yellow to green and blue where it has mixed out or been cooled. Fire smoke rises to the tunnel crown and spreads along it as a layer; against the airflow it creeps back (backlayering) until the fans reach critical velocity. This is an illustration of the behaviour, not CFD output. ILLUSTRATIVE
  13. EVIDENCE LABELS CODE a clause in a published standard · PRACTICE documented metro operating practice · INFERRED engineering inference, still to be confirmed · OPEN project-specific, deliberately not stated · ILLUSTRATIVE numbers chosen so the trends read.
  14. THE NUMBERSEvery temperature, speed and timing on this sheet is illustrative, and the incident clock runs at 6× speed. None of them is a design value. Real projects size tunnel ventilation with network simulation (SES-type models) and CFD against project criteria. Maintenance mode (damper tests and air purge at night) is not shown.
